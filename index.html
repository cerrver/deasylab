Why our work matters

Turning cancer's variability into better treatment

Cancer varies — from patient to patient, tumor to tumor, and cell to cell. The Deasy Lab treats that variability as information: we build models that read tumor biology from routinely acquired images, genomes, and clinical data, and turn it into decisions that make treatment more precise and less toxic. The aim is not modeling for its own sake, but tools that reach the clinic and change how patients are cared for.

Research programs

Four connected lines of work

The through-line across all of them: innovative mathematics used to understand patient and disease variability — and to turn it into a leverage point for cancer care.

01

Mathematical Oncology Initiative

Geometry & optimal transport for the structure of cancer

Breast Cancer Research Foundation · Simons Foundation

Geometry- and topology-based methods to subtype patients, integrate multi-omic networks, and surface therapeutic targets — and to train the next generation who build them.

optimal transportnetwork curvatureNetFlow
02

ECHO

Automated, clinically optimal treatment planning

2021 INFORMS Franz Edelman Laureate

A prioritized optimization that turns the hand-tuned trade-off between tumor coverage and organ sparing into a reproducible, mathematically optimal plan — now used to help plan roughly 23% of patients treated at MSK.

03

Proton & particle radiobiology

Modeling response across radiation modalities

A thread since the lab's first NIH award

Mechanistic models of how dose, timing, and tumor biology combine to determine response and toxicity when the physics differs from conventional photons — reproducing clinical observations across photon and carbon-ion therapy.

04

Digital twins

A simulatable model of each patient's tumor

Multimodality imaging + tumor biology

Combining FDG-PET and MRI with a mechanistic tumor-control model to forecast response and flag likely non-responders early — while treatment can still be adapted.

Software

CERR / pyCERR

An open-source platform, created in the lab, for relating medical images to radiotherapy dose and outcomes — registration, dose calculation, and quantitative ("radiomic") image analysis. It has grown past 250,000 lines of code, underpinned 70+ of the lab's studies, and been cited over 800 times, and is now re-implemented as pyCERR, a native Python version with a graphical interface. Development is led by Aditya Apte, PhD.

Apte et al., Medical Physics (2018), PMC6597320.

Discovery

A high-risk breast-cancer subtype found by computation

Using optimal-transport-based clustering of molecular data, the lab isolated a poor-prognosis, hypoxia-enriched subgroup of breast cancers and characterized its biology — showing that geometry-based methods can define clinically meaningful subtypes that conventional analyses miss.

Oh et al., npj Breast Cancer (2025), doi:10.1038/s41523-025-00725-y.
